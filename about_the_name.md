# About the name: Frances Glessner Lee

Glessner is named for Frances Glessner Lee (1878 to 1962), who is often called the mother of forensic science. She paid for Harvard's Department of Legal Medicine, built miniature death scenes to teach investigators how to look at a scene, and founded and ran the seminar that used them. The seminar still runs, and her models still train death investigators. This project borrows her name as an homage, and tries to earn it by following her ideas about how an investigator should work.

Glessner is an independent project. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by Glessner House, Harvard University, Harvard Associates in Police Science, the Maryland Office of the Chief Medical Examiner, or the Smithsonian.

The facts below come from the [sources](#sources) listed at the end. Where she described her own work, this page follows her 1952 article, "Legal Medicine at Harvard University" ([Journal of Criminal Law and Criminology 42(5)](https://scholarlycommons.law.northwestern.edu/jclc/vol42/iss5/12)).

## Her work

Frances Glessner was born in Chicago in 1878 into a family whose fortune came from International Harvester. She was taught at home by tutors and never went to college. Her brother's Harvard friend George Burgess Magrath, later the medical examiner for Boston, drew her into legal medicine: the use of medical knowledge to answer the law's questions about a death.

She came to it with a problem to solve. In 1952 she wrote that she had found legal medicine taught nowhere in America, so the people who needed it had nowhere to train. She endowed a Department of Legal Medicine at Harvard Medical School, founded in 1931 and active from 1938, and gave it the George Burgess Magrath Library in 1934. The department promoted trained medical examiners over elected coroners, who needed no training ([Countway Library, 2025](https://countway.harvard.edu/news/hms-department-legal-medicine-records-open-research)).

From about 1943 she built the Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death: about twenty dioramas at a scale of one inch to the foot, from a one-room shack to a three-room house. A carpenter built the rooms. She made the figures, clothing, and small details herself, down to knitted stockings and working locks ([Smithsonian American Art Museum](https://americanart.si.edu/exhibitions/nutshells)). In her words, no model reproduces a single case, but everything shown actually happened. The scenes include accidents, natural deaths, and deaths left undetermined because the original investigation was careless. They also show who the victims were: their circumstances, their money, their state of mind.

In 1945 she founded the Harvard Seminars in Homicide Investigation, week-long courses for state police by invitation, no more than two students from one force. Each student studied two Nutshells for about an hour and a half each, then gave a verbal report to the class. Only after the class discussed it was the point of each model disclosed. She called the models "an exercise in observing, interpreting, evaluating and reporting", with no solution to find. The investigator's real danger, she wrote, is the hunch: looking for and finding only the evidence that supports it.

The graduates formed Harvard Associates in Police Science, which met each year for a refresher course. In 1943 the New Hampshire State Police named her a captain and its educational director. Sources disagree on whether the post was honorary.

Her work outlived her. Eighteen of the Nutshells are on long-term loan from Harvard to the Maryland Office of the Chief Medical Examiner in Baltimore, where they are still used in training. The seminar she founded still runs there each year as the [Frances Glessner Lee Homicide Investigation Seminar](https://health.maryland.gov/ocme/Pages/Seminar.aspx), which the Maryland office calls the longest-running training program of its kind in the country. Its enrollment has widened to medical staff, attorneys, and private investigators. She hoped for that in 1952. In 2017 the Smithsonian's Renwick Gallery showed all nineteen known models together for the first time. Before the show, the Smithsonian's conservator found that decay had created false clues: a cracked window that looked like a break-in, and faded nail polish, used as blood, that changed how the stains read ([Washington Conservation Guild](https://washingtonconservationguild.org/2018/05/03/meeting-summary-crime-scene-conservation-preserving-nutshell-studies-unexplained-death/)).

## What Glessner takes from her

Glessner examines digital evidence rather than death scenes, and its scenes are timelines of event logs rather than rooms. The table pairs her practice with the part of the app that follows it. "Built" means it is in the app now. "Planned" means it is decided and not built yet.

| Her practice | In Glessner | Status |
|---|---|---|
| The whole scene, not only the body. Her models show the room, the household, and the victim's circumstances around the death. | One timeline across every host and every source, grouped into lanes, so an event is read beside everything around it. | Built |
| No "solution" to find. The models train observation; the investigator reaches the conclusion. | Guide, never decide. The app suggests next steps and says why, and the analyst accepts or rejects each one. It writes no finding and no verdict. Findings are records the analyst writes. | Built |
| Beware the hunch that finds only the evidence that fits. | State always visible: what is hidden, which clock, which window, each with a way back. Pivots show their counts before the click and flag broad ones. The Event page says what the evidence does not prove. | Built |
| Observe, interpret, evaluate, report: the student's report comes before the answer. | Marks and tags record what the analyst concluded, the journal records when and by whom, and the findings summary and audit trail report the method. | Built |
| Teach what to do, how, and why. She stressed the why. | Two analysts at once: full detail for the experienced analyst, and reason lines, hover hints, legends, and "show me" for the junior, so both understand what the app shows and why. | Principle set; pages being built |
| Every detail made exact, so the scene can be trusted. Decay later added false clues to her models. | Every evidence file is hashed at intake, inputs are re-checked after each tool run, and closing a case re-hashes the evidence. The journal is a hash chain, so a change to an earlier entry shows. | Built |
| The point of each model was disclosed only after the report. | Blind rounds: an analyst works a case without its answer key, the key is read only after the findings are recorded, and the findings are then scored against it. | Planned; no blind round has run yet |

## Training and education

Lee's lasting work was teaching, and the closest way to honor her is to make Glessner useful for teaching too. The ideas below are proposals, not plans.

- **Study cases.** An instructor packs a prepared case with a sealed answer key, as the Nutshells came with a point to disclose. The student works the case in Glessner without the key.
- **Report before reveal.** The key opens only after the student's findings are recorded. The journal's hash chain can show that the findings were written before the key was opened, which is the order Lee's seminars kept.
- **Compare after the reveal.** The app sets the student's marks beside the key's events: what was found, what was missed, and what was marked that the key says is benign. That turns a blind round's scoring into a feature.
- **Distractors by design.** Lee built details into her scenes that might or might not matter. A study case can include benign activity that resembles an attack, so the exercise trains judgment and not only search.
- **An optional time box.** Her students had about an hour and a half per model. A study case could carry a suggested time and record how long the student took.
- **A shared record of method.** The audit trail already lists every action in order, so an instructor can review how a student worked, not only what they concluded.

## Using her name with care

- The project uses the surname alone, Glessner. It uses no portrait of her, no images of the Nutshell Studies, and never her full name as an endorsement. Eighteen of the models belong to Harvard and are cared for by the Maryland Office of the Chief Medical Examiner.
- This page gives her rank as the record does: captain and educational director in the New Hampshire State Police, from 1943. Many sources call her the first woman police captain in the United States, but a 1917 record names a woman captain in the Dallas police ([Texas State Library](https://www.tsl.texas.gov/exhibits/suffrage/victory/jjfarley.html)), so this page doesn't repeat that claim.
- The project credits the institutions that keep her work alive: the Maryland Office of the Chief Medical Examiner, Harvard Associates in Police Science, Harvard's Countway Library, Glessner House in Chicago, and the Smithsonian American Art Museum.
- For more, read Corinne May Botz, *The Nutshell Studies of Unexplained Death* (2004), and Bruce Goldfarb, *18 Tiny Deaths* (2020).
